Recursive Rankings basketball

RankingsAboutFootball

The problem

Every team plays about thirty games out of three hundred and sixty-odd possible opponents, and almost all of them against the dozen or so teams in its own conference. Nobody plays a common schedule. So the raw question — who had the best season — has no direct answer. A 28‑4 record in a weak league and a 24‑8 in a brutal one are not comparable on their face.

The usual solutions all add information from outside the results. The AP and Coaches polls start from a preseason ballot written before a single game, and voters move teams up and down from there, carrying reputation with them. Most computer ratings lean on margin of victory, which rewards running up the score and punishes teams that empty the bench in the fourth quarter.

This ranking adds nothing. It knows who played whom and who won. That is all. No preseason poll, no margin of victory, no conference prestige, no human vote.

How it works

Start with a single game. If Duke beats Kansas, how much is that worth?

On its own, nothing — you cannot tell a good win from a bad one without knowing something about Kansas. So the system looks at what Kansas did in the rest of its season, with this one game removed so no result is ever counted twice against the team that produced it. If Kansas went on to win most of its other games, beating Kansas was worth a lot.

But that just moves the question along: how good were the teams Kansas beat? So it looks one step further out — the records of Kansas's other opponents, each with their own game against Kansas removed. That is the recursion. Your result, then your opponent's season, then their opponents' seasons.

Duke beat Kansas — Kansas was 20‑10 in its other games, and those thirty opponents were a combined .540 in theirs. Both numbers travel with the win.

Those grades are then sorted against each other, using the numbers in order: did you win, then how good was your opponent, then how good were theirs. Placing near the top scores near 100; near the bottom, near 0. A team's rating is the average of its games.

What a game is compared against

The slates view shows this directly: every result from one day, in the order the grader puts them, with its score beside it.

A grade on its own is not a number out of a hundred — it only becomes one once you say among what. By default that is every game played so far this season, up to whichever day you are viewing. The pool grows as the season does, and a game is re‑scored against the larger pool each day.

The original method compared a game only against the games played the same day, and you can switch back to it under the gear icon. It has two problems the season‑wide pool does not. The first is that an identical result is worth different amounts depending on how busy the calendar happened to be that night — which no team controls. Basketball feels this harder than football does: a night with thirty games and a Saturday with two hundred are both “a day”, so the field a result is measured against swings enormously.

The second problem is worse, because it is a contradiction rather than a calibration error. Two of a team's own results fall on different days, so their percentiles come from different populations — and they can therefore come out in the wrong order, with the grader saying one win was better and the score saying the other was. Across the five seasons here there are 901,439 pairs of a team's own games that the grader ranks strictly; day‑by‑day scoring reverses 36,994 of them — one pair in twenty‑four, noticeably worse than football's one in thirty‑seven, because the buckets are smaller and vary more. Pooling reverses none, because a single population cannot disagree with the comparison that built it.

Football buckets by week; basketball has to bucket by day, because teams play two or three times a week and the model holds one game per team per bucket. Weekly buckets would quietly throw away about 45% of the season. No team has played twice in a day in any season here.

Which games the field is drawn from

The grader has no notion of divisions, and a record only means something relative to the teams it was earned against. Where a corner of the schedule never meets D1, its records are measured on a different ruler and should not be sorted into one list with D1 results. Basketball is largely spared this: the feed carries D1 games and D1 games against outside opposition, and no games between two non‑D1 teams, so games with a D1 team in them and every game played pick out the same set. Both are offered under the gear icon so they stay distinguishable if that ever changes.

The third option, games between two D1 teams, does bite: it stops the 8.8% of results that cross the line from counting toward a rating at all, which can leave a team rated on slightly fewer games. Either way the grading is untouched — an opponent’s record is still its real record, taken from all of its games. Only the field a result is placed in changes. Football, where three‑quarters of all results involve no top‑division team, is affected far more; the football page works through why.

Rankings change in hindsight

Because a game is graded by what happened afterwards, the value of a November result is not settled in November. It shifts every time one of the teams involved plays again. A win over a team that then collapses quietly loses value; a win over a team that goes on a run gains it. Early-season games get revised the most, because the most basketball happens after them.

Move the date slider and you are not just adding games — you are watching the whole season get reinterpreted.

Weighing the opponent

The score so far says where a result finished among everyone who played that day. That already leans on opponent quality, but weakly. Beating a team that happens to be unbeaten counts enormously, whoever that team turns out to be — and an unbeaten record against poor opposition is still unbeaten.

So there is an option to scale each game by how good the opponent actually proved to be. A win over the twelfth‑best team keeps almost all its value; the same win over the hundred‑and‑twelfth keeps very little. The same scaling runs over defeats, and there the anchor has to be mirrored. The win/loss line sits at exactly 50, because every game puts one win and one loss into the pool; 50 is therefore the floor of the win range and the ceiling of the loss range. Shrink everything toward 50 and you discount a cheap win and forgive a cheap loss in the same stroke. So each half is pulled toward its own worst end instead: a win toward 50, a loss toward 0. Beating nobody earns nearly nothing; losing to nobody is as bad as a result gets, and every win still finishes above every loss. Across basketball 2026 that widens the gap between beating a top‑25 team and beating one outside the top 100 from 27 points to 30, and the equivalent gap for defeats from 29 to 30 rather than collapsing it. Each game's score on a team page carries the full arithmetic on hover.

A 7‑0 team beat a 5‑0 opponent, which scored 98.6 — among the very best results of that day. But that opponent's own five wins came against teams at 2‑5, 2‑4, 2‑4, 2‑3 and 1‑4. Weighted, the win is worth 79.6: still good, no longer extraordinary.

It is switched on by default, in the balanced setting. The ranking already accounts for opponent quality once, in the two levels described above, and weighting applies it a second time — a modelling choice rather than a plain reading of the results. The trade is a sharper ranking that sits further from the raw evidence. Switching it off gives the plainer thing: what the season says, with as little laid on top as possible. Both are worth looking at, and the gear icon moves between them.

There is also a catch, and it is the interesting part. The weight depends on how good the opponent is, which is what the ranking is trying to work out. The ranking therefore feeds back into its own inputs, so it is not calculated once but solved: rank, re‑weigh, rank again, until the numbers stop moving. That usually takes about a dozen passes. The page says how many it took, and says so plainly if it fails to settle.

Three settings:

  • Off leaves the raw figures alone.
  • Balanced (the default) pulls each half toward its own worst end: a win toward the halfway mark, a loss toward zero. A soft win is pulled back toward the middle rather than beneath a defeat, and a soft loss is pushed down rather than forgiven.
  • Strong scales the score itself. It bites harder, and it breaks that rule: a good loss can finish above a poor win.

How the defaults were chosen

Four of the settings have a default, and those four were picked by measurement rather than taste. Every combination — two ways of scoring, three fields, three weighting modes — was run against the AP and Coaches polls across all seasons here, at every week a poll was published, and the one that tracked the voters most closely won. That is season‑so‑far scoring, games with a D1 team, and balanced weighting, at a mean Spearman of 0.571. Because this feed holds no games between two non‑D1 teams, every‑game scoring is the same set and ties it exactly; the nearest different setting is games between two D1 teams, at 0.567. The same combination wins on the football side, measured separately.

It is worth being honest about what that does and does not justify. The margins are narrow — a few thousandths separate the leading options, and the difference between any of them is far smaller than the swing from one season, or one week of a season, to the next. So the default is the best general guess, not a settled answer, and on any particular season another setting may well read better. There is also something a little circular about tuning a poll‑free ranking to agree with polls; the measure is used here only to break ties between defensible options, never to decide what the system counts as evidence. Every option stays under the gear icon, and the correlation figures at the top of the page update as you change them, so you can check any of this yourself.

The victors gate

A pure record-based system has a known weakness: a team can go undefeated against opposition that never tested it, and the maths has no way to say so. An unbeaten record looks the same whoever produced it.

The gate is a separate check applied after the ranking is computed: to be listed at all, you must have beaten somebody worth beating — and that team must qualify too. The rule is applied over and over until the set stops changing, so nobody holds a place on a win over a team the rule itself rejects.

It tightens as the season fills in, because it needs cross‑conference results to work. Early on, teams have mostly played inside their own conference and no chain of justification can close; run the rule in September and it returns nobody at all.

  • The first fortnight — off. Nothing closes that early; run the rule in mid‑November and it returns nobody at all.
  • From about day 20 (late November) — the top 300.
  • From day 30 (early December) — the top 200.
  • From day 60 (early January) — the top 100.
  • From day 100 (mid‑February) — the top 50.

You can override all of this from the control at the top of the rankings, which starts on automatic. One pass is a looser version that reads the tier off the finished order once without re-checking, and the tier itself can be widened or narrowed.

The gate never changes a rating or an order. It only decides who is shown, and excluded teams still count as opponents for everyone else. A team can lose its place because an opponent slipped, and get it back the week after — that is the rule working, not a glitch.

What it cannot do

Being honest about this matters more than the rankings do.

  • It is a record of what happened, not a forecast. The voters' preseason ballot encodes real information — returning players, recruiting, coaching — that this system refuses on principle and pays for early. Through November it knows very little, and says so by ranking almost nobody.
  • Every game counts the same. A November exhibition against a low‑major is weighed equally with a February road game at a rival, so a team that pads its non‑conference schedule is marked down for it even though the schedule says nothing about how good that team is. This is the system's largest blind spot.
  • No loss ever outranks a win. Quality of defeat is measured — a loss to one of the best teams in the country scores far higher than a loss to a bad one, a spread of tens of points. But the result comes first in the ordering, so every win outscores every loss: the best loss of that entire season was worth 49.9 and the worst win 50.1. A team with two good losses therefore takes two trips into the bottom half that an unbeaten team with a weak schedule never takes, which is why strong two‑loss teams can finish below weaker unbeaten ones.
  • No home field, no injuries, no weather, no context.

A note on the data

Five seasons are here, 2021‑22 through 2025‑26. Each covers the regular season and conference tournaments up to Selection Sunday. The NCAA tournament itself is not included: this ranks the season that earns a bid, not the tournament that follows.

Teams outside Division I appear only as opponents. They are never ranked, but beating one still counts — and counts for very little, which is the point.

Prior work

Ranking teams from wins and losses alone is a well-worked problem. The Colley Matrix, one of the computer rankings used by college football's BCS, is built on the same refusal to use margin of victory, and the same ideas run through basketball's own RPI and the NET. Park and Newman's network-based ranking (2005) counts direct and indirect wins through the season's win graph, much as the levels here do. The idea that later results should revise earlier estimates is called smoothing, and has been applied to games at least since Glickman (1999) and in Whole-History Rating (2008).

What is unusual here is the presentation: keeping every intermediate state of the season rather than only the final answer, and the self-consistent gate.
