Recursive Rankings football

RankingsAboutBasketball

The problem

Every team plays about twelve games out of roughly a hundred and thirty possible opponents. Nobody plays a common schedule. So the raw question — who had the best season — has no direct answer. An 11‑1 record against weak opposition and a 9‑3 against brutal opposition are not comparable on their face.

The usual solutions all add information from outside the results. The AP and Coaches polls start from a preseason ballot written before a single game, and voters move teams up and down from there, carrying reputation with them. Most computer ratings lean on margin of victory, which rewards running up the score and punishes teams that empty the bench in the fourth quarter.

This ranking adds nothing. It knows who played whom and who won. That is all. No preseason poll, no margin of victory, no conference prestige, no human vote.

How it works

Start with a single game. If Florida beats Auburn, how much is that worth?

On its own, nothing — you cannot tell a good win from a bad one without knowing something about Auburn. So the system looks at what Auburn did in the rest of its season, with this one game removed so no result is ever counted twice against the team that produced it. If Auburn went on to win most of its other games, beating Auburn was worth a lot.

But that just moves the question along: how good were the teams Auburn beat? So it looks one step further out — the records of Auburn's other opponents, each with their own game against Auburn removed. That is the recursion. Your result, then your opponent's season, then their opponents' seasons.

Florida beat Auburn — Auburn was 4‑2 in its other games, and those six opponents were a combined .667 in theirs. Both numbers travel with the win.

Those grades are then sorted against each other, using the numbers in order: did you win, then how good was your opponent, then how good were theirs. Placing near the top scores near 100; near the bottom, near 0. A team's rating is the average of its games.

What a game is compared against

The slates view shows this directly: every result from one week, in the order the grader puts them, with its score beside it.

A grade on its own is not a number out of a hundred — it only becomes one once you say among what. By default that is every game played so far this season, up to whichever week you are viewing. The pool grows as the season does, and a game is re‑scored against the larger pool each week.

The original method compared a game only against the games played in the same week, and you can switch back to it under the gear icon. It has two problems the season‑wide pool does not. The first is that an identical result is worth different amounts depending on what else happened to be on the schedule that week — which no team controls. Across 2025 that distortion was small in the ordinary weeks, about nine‑tenths of a point, but championship weekend had only sixty‑four games in it and deflated every result by nearly sixteen points, hitting exactly the games that decide a playoff field.

The second problem is worse, because it is a contradiction rather than a calibration error. Two of a team's own results sit in different weeks, so their percentiles come from different populations — and they can therefore come out in the wrong order. Florida beat Auburn in Week 4 and Texas in Week 8; both were wins over teams that finished .800 in their other games, and Texas’s opponents had the better seasons of the two, so the Texas win is strictly the better result. Yet week‑by‑week scoring put Auburn above it, 95.6 to 95.1. Pooled, the order is right: 95.3 for Texas, 95.2 for Auburn. Across all thirteen seasons here there are 284,676 pairs of a team's own games that the grader ranks strictly; week‑by‑week scoring reverses 7,762 of them — one pair in thirty‑seven. Pooling reverses none, because a single population cannot disagree with the comparison that built it.

Pooling is not free. It agrees with the final AP poll very slightly less often — a mean Spearman correlation of 0.78 against 0.79 across 2014–2026, which is well inside the year‑to‑year noise, better in five seasons and clearly worse in two. The rankings themselves barely move: about one place on average among the teams shown, and the same teams pass the gate.

Which games the field is drawn from

The grader has no notion of divisions. It sees wins, losses and who played whom — that is the point of it — but there is a consequence. In week 8 of 2026 a D3 team, Maryville, beat an unbeaten Trinity (TX) and produced the vector 1 · 1.000 · 0.660. Ohio State beat an unbeaten Indiana and produced 1 · 1.000 · 0.672. Near‑identical, and the slate duly ranked them first and second.

But they are not comparable. A record only means something relative to the teams it was earned against, and these two sets of teams never meet. Maryville’s opponents, and its opponents’ opponents — the entire span the vector measures — contain no FBS team at all. Ohio State’s contain thirty‑one. The two 1.000s are measured on different rulers.

That would be harmless if such results stayed out of the way, but they do not. Through week 8 of 2026, 77% of all results involve no FBS team, and they hold 74 of the best 100 places. A score is a position, so an FBS team’s number was being set largely by where it fell among games it could never have played. The field is therefore drawn, by default, from games with an FBS team in them: every game an FBS team actually played, lower divisions included, and nothing else. The gear icon can tighten that to games between two FBS teams, in which a cross‑division result neither scores a team nor sits in the field, or loosen it all the way back to every game played.

The grading itself is untouched by the choice. A lower‑division opponent’s record is still its real record, taken from all of its games; only the field a result is placed in changes. The effect is not small — narrowing it moves the 2026 table by 2.5 places on average and as much as 12 — and it has nothing to do with pooling, which shows the same thing under week‑by‑week scoring.

Rankings change in hindsight

Because a game is graded by what happened afterwards, the value of a Week 2 result is not settled in Week 2. It shifts every time one of the teams involved plays again. A win over a team that then collapses quietly loses value; a win over a team that goes on a run gains it. Early-season games get revised the most, because the most football happens after them.

Move the week slider and you are not just adding games — you are watching the whole season get reinterpreted.

Each team's page draws that out: a line of where the team stood after every week, with the AP and Coaches polls on the same axes, and the selection committee’s ranking from the point in the season it starts. The shapes differ in a way worth looking at. The voters start from a preseason ballot and move in small steps; this starts from nothing and can swing violently in September before settling. In 2025 it had Indiana first by week 7; the AP took until week 16 to agree.

Weighing the opponent

The score so far says where a result finished among everyone who played that week. That already leans on opponent quality, but weakly. Beating a team that happens to be unbeaten counts enormously, whoever that team turns out to be — and an unbeaten record against poor opposition is still unbeaten.

So there is an option to scale each game by how good the opponent actually proved to be. A win over the twelfth‑best team keeps almost all its value; the same win over the hundred‑and‑twelfth keeps very little. The same scaling runs over defeats, and there the anchor has to be mirrored. The win/loss line sits at exactly 50, because every game puts one win and one loss into the pool; 50 is therefore the floor of the win range and the ceiling of the loss range. Shrink everything toward 50 and you discount a cheap win and forgive a cheap loss in the same stroke. So each half is pulled toward its own worst end instead: a win toward 50, a loss toward 0. Beating nobody earns nearly nothing; losing to nobody is as bad as a result gets, and every win still finishes above every loss. Across football 2026 that widens the gap between beating a top‑25 team and beating one outside the top 100 from 31 points to 36, and holds the equivalent gap for defeats at 33 rather than collapsing it. Each game's score on a team page carries the full arithmetic on hover.

A 7‑0 team beat a 5‑0 opponent, which scored 98.3 — among the very best results the season has produced. But that opponent's own five wins came against teams at 2‑5, 2‑4, 2‑4, 2‑3 and 1‑4. Weighted, the win is worth 79.8: still good, no longer extraordinary.

It is switched on by default, in the balanced setting. The ranking already accounts for opponent quality once, in the two levels described above, and weighting applies it a second time — a modelling choice rather than a plain reading of the results. The trade is a sharper ranking that sits further from the raw evidence. Switching it off gives the plainer thing: what the season says, with as little laid on top as possible. Both are worth looking at, and the gear icon moves between them.

There is also a catch, and it is the interesting part. The weight depends on how good the opponent is, which is what the ranking is trying to work out. The ranking therefore feeds back into its own inputs, so it is not calculated once but solved: rank, re‑weigh, rank again, until the numbers stop moving. That usually takes about a dozen passes. The page says how many it took, and says so plainly if it fails to settle.

Three settings:

  • Off leaves the raw figures alone.
  • Balanced (the default) pulls each half toward its own worst end: a win toward the halfway mark, a loss toward zero. A soft win is pulled back toward the middle rather than beneath a defeat, and a soft loss is pushed down rather than forgiven.
  • Strong scales the score itself. It bites harder, and it breaks that rule: a good loss can finish above a poor win.

How the defaults were chosen

Four of the settings have a default, and those four were picked by measurement rather than taste. Every combination — two ways of scoring, three fields, three weighting modes — was run against the AP and Coaches polls across all thirteen seasons here, at every week a poll was published — 358 paired measurements — and the one that tracked the voters most closely won. That is season‑so‑far scoring, games with an FBS team, and balanced weighting, at a mean Spearman of 0.605 against 0.601 for the next best and 0.593 for the field it replaced. It leads at most weeks of the season, early and late alike, so the choice is not an artefact of when it was measured. The same combination wins on the basketball side, measured separately.

It is worth being honest about what that does and does not justify. The margins are narrow: the top six settings sit within 0.012 of each other, while agreement swings from 0.30 in 2020 to 0.73 in 2022 between seasons, and six different combinations each win at least one season outright. So the default is the best general guess, not a settled answer, and on any particular season another setting may well read better. There is also something a little circular about tuning a poll‑free ranking to agree with polls; the measure is used here only to break ties between defensible options, never to decide what the system counts as evidence. Every option stays under the gear icon, and the correlation figures at the top of the page update as you change them, so you can check any of this yourself.

The victors gate

A pure record-based system has a known weakness: a team can go undefeated against opposition that never tested it, and the maths has no way to say so. An unbeaten record looks the same whoever produced it.

The gate is a separate check applied after the ranking is computed: to be listed at all, you must have beaten somebody worth beating. Through the middle of the season that team must qualify too, the rule repeating until the set stops changing, so nobody holds a place on a win over a team the rule itself rejects.

It tightens as the season fills in, because it needs cross‑conference results to work. Early on, teams have mostly played inside their own conference and no chain of justification can close; run the rule in September and it returns nobody at all.

  • Before week 8 — off. There isn't enough football yet.
  • Weeks 8–9 — you must have beaten someone in the top 100 who also qualifies.
  • Weeks 10–14 — the top 75.
  • Week 15 onward — you must have beaten someone in the top 25, checked once rather than repeated.

Why the last one is checked only once

By the final stretch the question has become who belongs in the playoff conversation, and the honest test is whether you beat somebody already in it. But that test cannot be iterated at so tight a tier, because of what a self‑consistent set is: every member needs an edge into the set, which forces a cycle somewhere, and an undefeated team cannot be in a cycle — nobody beat it.

Run over the top 25 until it converges, the 2023 season returns seven teams, five of them Big 12 sides who beat one another. Michigan is not among them. Michigan went 13‑0, beat the teams this system ranks 6th, 10th and 18th, and won the national title; all three of those victims are dropped on earlier passes, so Michigan's qualifying wins vanish and it falls too. The rule punishes dominance and rewards circular mediocrity. Across twelve seasons it leaves fewer than twelve teams in three of them, and ejects the eventual champion repeatedly.

Checked once, the same question behaves. It never collapses, never drops a champion in any season here, and agrees with the committee's twelve slightly more often than the self‑consistent rule over the top 50 it replaced — 9.8 against 9.6 of twelve.

You can override all of this from the control at the top of the rankings, which starts on automatic. One pass is a looser version that reads the tier off the finished order once without re-checking, and the tier itself can be widened or narrowed.

The gate never changes a rating or an order. It only decides who is shown, and excluded teams still count as opponents for everyone else. A team can lose its place because an opponent slipped, and get it back the week after — that is the rule working, not a glitch.

Does it predict?

The backtest asks a question the ranking was never built to answer. Rank the teams on weeks 1 to N, predict every week N+1 game between two FBS sides by taking the higher‑ranked team, and score it against what happened. Over all thirteen seasons that is about seven thousand games, none of which the ranking had seen when it made the call.

It is a fair test because the ordering at week N genuinely uses nothing after week N. That is worth checking rather than assuming, given the whole system is built on hindsight: engines built on seasons truncated at week N produce identical orderings to the full-season engine evaluated at N, across ten thousand comparisons without exception.

It does carry predictive content. It beats plain win percentage on the games the two disagree about, and it beats its own unweighted self, both at better than one‑in‑twenty odds of being chance. It beats picking the home team by a distance.

It also loses to the AP poll, 70‑38 of the 108 games the two both called and disagreed about. That is a real result and it belongs on the page. Voters are allowed to know things a record does not contain — an injured quarterback, a side that has stopped trying, a result everyone could see was a fluke — and this has only ever looked at who beat whom. The sample is small, because a comparison needs both to rank both teams.

Two cautions on reading that table. The hit rates sit on different sets of games and cannot be compared straight down the column: each method declines any game it cannot call, and the AP, ranking twenty‑five teams, is tested almost entirely on games between ranked sides, which are the close ones. The head‑to‑head column is the honest comparison. And none of this is the job the ranking is for — ordering teams by what they have earned is a different question from forecasting what they will do next, and a modest score here is not by itself a fault.

What it cannot do

Being honest about this matters more than the rankings do.

  • It is a record of what happened, not a forecast. Tested against the following week's games it trails the human polls badly through September and October, and only catches up around week 11. The voters' preseason ballot encodes real information — returning players, recruiting, coaching — that this system refuses on principle and pays for early.
  • Every game counts the same. A tune-up against a weak opponent is weighed equally with a conference decider, so a team that schedules three easy non-conference games is marked down for it even though the schedule says nothing about how good that team is. This is the system's largest blind spot.
  • No loss ever outranks a win. Quality of defeat is measured — losing to a top‑10 team scored an average of 46.8 across the 2025 season, losing to a team outside the top 90 scored 6.2, a spread of more than forty points. But the result comes first in the ordering, so every win outscores every loss: the best loss of that entire season was worth 49.9 and the worst win 50.1. A team with two good losses therefore takes two trips into the bottom half that an unbeaten team with a weak schedule never takes, which is why strong two‑loss teams can finish below weaker unbeaten ones.
  • No home field, no injuries, no weather, no context.

A note on the data

Thirteen seasons are here, 2014 through the current one — the span of the College Football Playoff. Conference championship games are included; they are ordinary games with ordinary results, and they are often the ones that decide who plays in January. Bowl games and the playoff itself are not: this ranks the season that earns a place, not the tournament that follows.

A finished season opens on selection week, not on its last week. The playoff field is chosen the day after the conference championships, and the season runs on past that — 2025 has a week 17 holding eleven games and no poll at all, played when the field was already set. Since the question this ranking exists to answer is who had earned a place, it opens where that was decided. The slider still reaches the end of the season; only the starting point moved. The current season opens on its newest data instead.

Three rankings are carried alongside for comparison: the AP and Coaches polls, which run from preseason to the end, and the CFP ranking from the selection committee, which only starts publishing around week 10. The committee’s is the one with something at stake, since it sets the playoff field. Its predecessor, the BCS standings, ran from 1998 to 2013 and so falls entirely outside the seasons here — the playoff era and the BCS era do not overlap by a single season.

2020 was shortened by the pandemic. Some teams played three or four games, conferences kept separate schedules, and whole regions barely met. The rankings for that year are honest about what happened but are built on far less evidence than any other season.

Prior work

Ranking teams from wins and losses alone is a well-worked problem. The Colley Matrix, one of the computer rankings used by the BCS, is built on the same refusal to use margin of victory. Park and Newman's network-based ranking (2005) counts direct and indirect wins through the season's win graph, much as the levels here do. The idea that later results should revise earlier estimates is called smoothing, and has been applied to games at least since Glickman (1999) and in Whole-History Rating (2008).

What is unusual here is the presentation: keeping every intermediate state of the season rather than only the final answer, and the self-consistent gate.
