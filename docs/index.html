Settings

Compare each game against

Every game is graded first, then scored by where that grade places it among other games — so the set it is compared against decides the number. The season so far pools every game played up to the week you are viewing, which lets a team’s own results be ranked against each other. That week alone compares a game only with the games played beside it, so the same win scores lower in a crowded week than a thin one, and two of a team’s wins can come out in the wrong order.

Score against

The grader has no notion of divisions. A D3 team beating an unbeaten D3 team produces the same three numbers as Ohio State beating an unbeaten Indiana — but records only compare across teams with overlapping opponents, and a D3 team’s two levels out reach no FBS team at all, so the two are measured on different rulers. Through week 8 of 2026, 77% of all results involve no FBS team, and they hold 74 of the best 100 places. Games with an FBS team keeps every game an FBS team actually played, lower divisions included, and drops only the ones it had nothing to do with. Games between FBS teams goes further and stops cross‑division results counting toward a rating at all, which can leave a team rated on very few games. Every game played is the original behaviour. Excluded games still count in their own teams’ records either way — only the measuring stick changes.

Victors gate over top

To be listed at all you must have beaten somebody worth beating, and that team must qualify too — the rule repeats until the set stops changing. It stays off until week 8 and tightens as the season fills in, because it needs cross‑conference results to work. It never changes a rating or an order; it only decides who is shown.

Opponent weighting

Scales every game by how good the opponent turned out to be, so a win over a poor team counts for less and a loss to a good one costs less. Balanced keeps every win above every loss; strong bites harder and drops that rule. Off by default: the ranking already weighs opponents once, and this does it a second time.

How this works

What this is. Every game is graded by what happened next: your result, then how your opponent did across the rest of its season with this game removed, then how their opponents did in turn. That grade is turned into a 0–100 score by where it places among every game played so far this season, and a team's rating is the average of its games. No preseason poll, no margin of victory, no votes. (The gear icon can score each game against its own week alone instead — the original method.)

Why rankings move backwards. A Week 2 win is not settled in Week 2. It gains value if the team you beat keeps winning and loses value if they collapse. Drag the week slider and you are watching the season reinterpret itself. Click any team to see where it has stood after every week, with the two polls alongside, and then each of its games and how their worth has shifted: the small line beside each game is that game alone, re‑graded after every week since it was played. Hover it for the week‑by‑week figures.

Seeing the raw order. Slates lays out every result from a single week in exactly the order the grader puts them — wins above losses, then by opponent — with both opponent numbers and the score beside each one. The ranking is just those scores averaged per team, so this is the table everything else is built from.

The victors gate. To be listed you must have beaten somebody worth beating, and that team must qualify too — the rule is applied over and over until the set stops changing. It needs enough cross‑conference play to work, so it stays off until week 8 and tightens as the season fills in. It never changes a rating or an order; it only decides who is shown.
