Recursive rankings

Every game is graded by what happened next. A Week 1 result is scored by how your opponent did in Week 2, which is scored by how their opponent did in Week 3, and so on to the end of the season. Nothing is assumed. No preseason poll, no margin of victory, no human vote — only results, read backward from the last week played.

National ranking

The slates rank teams within a week. To get one national order, each team's weekly slate finish is converted to a 0–100 score and combined across the season.

Against the human polls

This is the ranking, in its own order, with the voters alongside. The AP and Coaches polls carry preseason expectation, reputation, margin of victory and the eye test — none of which this system can see, so every disagreement is a place one of them knows something the other does not. Top‑division only, and the poll shown is always the one published off the same games the slider is showing.

Team detail

Does the ranking predict games it hasn't seen?

Rank every team using weeks 1 through N, then look at week N+1 and check whether the higher-ranked team actually won. Repeat for every week of the season. This owes nothing to anyone's opinion — it asks only whether the ordering carries real information about what happens next.

Coin flip is 50%. Home advantage alone is usually around 60%. A ranking that is doing real work should beat both, and should beat plain win percentage — otherwise the recursion is not earning its complexity.

Games where either team is unranked, or where the two are exactly tied, are skipped rather than guessed.

The significance column compares each method against plain win percentage on the games they both predicted. Only disagreements count: if two methods pick the same winner, that game tells you nothing about which is better. A gap can look large in the totals and still be noise.

What did it cost to be this good?

Estimated roster budgets — mostly NIL and revenue-share spend to assemble the current roster — set against each team's national ranking score. Budgets are published as ranges; the midpoint is used for the per-point figure. Coverage is limited to the schools estimates exist for.

Source. Budget figures are estimates of total roster cost (NIL collective plus revenue share, where applicable) gathered from public reporting, not official disclosures — treat the ranges as ballpark, not audited.

$ / point divides the budget midpoint by the team's national score for the selected aggregation, so a lower number means more ranking value per dollar. It says nothing about causation — a team's rating already reflects last season's actual results, which spending helped produce, but plenty else did too.

Format for your loader script

This is the contract. Have your script emit exactly this and the app will pick it up. teams is optional — anything appearing in a game is registered automatically.

{
  "teams": [
    { "name": "Alabama", "conference": "SEC",      "division": "FBS" },
    { "name": "Mercer",  "conference": "Southern", "division": "FCS" }
  ],
  "games": [
    { "week": 1, "home": "Alabama", "away": "Mercer",
      "homePoints": 48, "awayPoints": 10 }
  ]
}

A game may instead be written {"week":1,"winner":"Alabama","loser":"Mercer"}. Scores decide who won; point differential is used only to break ties in the national ranking. Games with equal scores, missing scores, or a team playing twice in one week are skipped and reported below.

Division handling

How games involving lower-division teams enter the math. The National tab separately controls whether they appear in the table.

Connectivity

The ranking compares teams through the games that link them. Where two groups barely play each other, the system cannot tell that one is stronger — it can only see how teams did inside their own group. Low numbers here mean the corresponding ranking should be read with suspicion.

Known limits

Weak groups get flattered. In testing on generated seasons, teams in the weakest conference finished about 26 places higher than they deserved and the strongest conference about 19 places lower. The bias shrinks as non-conference play rises, which is the same connectivity effect measured above.

Easy opponents pay. Teams that scheduled two lower-division opponents gained roughly 15 places. Two separate causes: the win itself counts nearly in full, and a lower-division team's record looks strong because it was earned against other lower-division teams. The division options above address the second; nothing in a pure win-loss system addresses the first.

Nothing here is a scoring bug. Every game is one indistinguishable bit, so the only evidence about the gap between two groups is the count of games between them, and there is rarely enough. Margin of victory is the standard remedy, at the cost of the purity this system was built for.

Byes — skip forward. If the next team in a chain is idle that week, the chain advances to their next actual game rather than breaking. Every bit stays a real result.

Chains that run out. A team whose season ends mid-chain contributes a neutral mark, which sorts between a win and a loss. It neither rewards nor punishes.

Ties are permanent. Two chains that merge onto the same team share every remaining bit. If they were tied when they merged, no further draft separates them. The app reports how many teams are still tied.

Sum vs average is the one place games-played is priced. Sum rewards playing more, which matches the instinct that a bye shouldn't be free. Average normalizes it. Both are one click apart.
